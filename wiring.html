Wiring

One ESP8266, three peripherals, eleven usable pins. Every one is spoken for, and two of them have a trap on them.

ESP-12F · ST7796S · MCP2515 · MPU-6050

Power: the panel runs from 5 V (it has its own regulator and level shifters). The MPU-6050 breakout takes 3V3 or 5 V — use 3V3, because its I²C lines then sit at 3V3 and go straight to the ESP. The CAN board must be a 3V3 one; that is the next section.

Trap 1 — the blue MCP2515 board will kill the ESP8266

The common module pairs an MCP2515 with a TJA1050 transceiver, and the TJA1050 needs 5 V. The whole board therefore runs at 5 V, so its SO pin drives about 4.5 V straight into GPIO12, which is a 3.3 V input. It may appear to work for a while. It is out of spec from the first frame.

Buy the SN65HVD230 version instead — sold as “SN65HVD230 CAN board” or CJMCU-230. That transceiver is a 3.3 V part, the whole module runs at 3V3, and nothing needs shifting. It is the same money.

If you already have a TJA1050 board, the usual fix is to cut the MCP2515’s VCC from the 5 V rail and feed it 3V3 while the transceiver keeps 5 V. That works, but it is a scalpel job on a board that costs less than the blade.

Trap 2 — do not put the CAN chip select on GPIO16

GPIO16 is not on the normal GPIO block; it is the RTC pin XPD_DCDC. Espressif’s datasheet is explicit: “XPD_DCDC can only be configured with internal pull-down, other GPIO PAD can only be configured with internal pull-up.”

A chip select must idle high, and this is the one pin on the board that cannot be held there by anything but software. The MCP2515’s CS lived here for months and the controller never answered once — every register read came back FF, on wiring that measured perfect. What settled it was coryjfowler’s loopback example with nothing else connected and CS on D8: the chip configured, sent a frame to itself, and read it back. SPI in both directions, the bit timing and the crystal, all working. The module was never the problem.

So the chip select is on D8/GPIO15 now, and GPIO16 drives the backlight instead — the one job on this board that does not care about pull-ups or interrupts. PWM does work there: analogWrite accepts any pin up to 16 and the core’s waveform generator carries an explicit pin == 16 path.

The MCP2515’s INT pin is deliberately not wired. The only pin left would be GPIO16, and GPIO16 cannot raise an interrupt at all, so it would mean polling a pin instead of polling a register — strictly worse, because the register read also tells you which buffer is full.

Orientation: flat, chip facing up

Yaw rate — how fast the car is turning — is measured about the vertical axis. On the MPU-6050 that is +Z, which points straight out of the top face of the chip (InvenSense’s own orientation figure, with the pin-1 dot on the top face). So the board must lie roughly flat, component side up, in the plane of the dash.

Screw or tape it down properly. It does not have to be level, and it does not have to be square to the car — but it does have to be rigid. A sensor that can rock in its mount measures the rocking.

It levels itself at boot

Your dash slopes, so “flat” is never level, and reading raw Z under-reads by cos(tilt): 1.5 % at 10°, 6 % at 20°, 13 % at 30° — plus real yaw leaks onto the axis you are not reading.

So the firmware measures the mounting angle once at start-up, from gravity, and rotates every gyro sample into vehicle axes with the standard Euler-rate relation ψ̇ = (q·sinφ + r·cosφ) / cosθ. That means you only have to get it roughly flat. It is done at boot and never repeated while driving, on purpose: braking and cornering tilt the measured gravity vector exactly when the yaw rate matters most.

One consequence — have the car parked on level-ish ground when you power it up, and do not remount the sensor without a power cycle.

The zero point is handled the same way. A gyro’s bias is huge (InvenSense quote ±20 °/s at 25 °C, and the same ±20 °/s of wander across temperature), so calibrating once at power-on is nowhere near enough. The firmware re-zeros every time the car is provably standing still — and it now takes that from the CAN speed, not the phone, so it works in a tunnel, in a car park, and with nothing plugged in.

Check the crystal on the board with your eyes before you flash. Most are 8 MHz, most library examples assume 16, and a mismatch is completely silent — you simply never receive a frame and there is nothing to see. It is CAN_XTAL_MHZ at the top of hud_can.h.

Do not add termination. The car’s bus is already terminated at both ends; a third 120 Ω across it drags the differential voltage down and can take the whole powertrain bus off the air. Every one of these modules ships with one fitted — there is a jumper or a resistor marked R1/120R to remove.

The bus itself

Your five IDs are PT-CAN, which on an E60 runs at 500 kbit/s (K-CAN, the body bus, is 100 kbit/s — wrong bus, wrong speed, nothing decodes). Where PT-CAN appears on the OBD connector varies by year and market on the E6x, so verify before you trust a diagram: tap a pair, set the firmware to 500 k, and watch for $CAN,ok followed by frames. If nothing arrives, try the other pair before you start doubting the wiring.

The firmware never transmits — and here is why that matters

The MCP2515 is put in listen-only mode, which does not even acknowledge frames, so it cannot disturb the bus however wrong its bit timing is.

But there is a consequence your notes did not have. Microchip’s datasheet, section 10.3: “In Listen-Only mode, both valid and invalid messages will be received, regardless of filters and masks.” The six hardware filters do nothing in this mode. Every frame on a 500 kbit/s bus lands in two RX buffers about 250 µs deep, and the sorting happens in software.

That is fine — the speed counter is cumulative so dropped frames cost nothing, and the rest are absolute values that refresh within 100 ms. But it is measured rather than assumed: the board reports $CANDROP,<overflows>,<frames> every five seconds. If that first number climbs while you drive, the loop is spending too long painting, not the wiring being wrong.

One line in the display config that is not optional

The display and the CAN controller share the SPI bus at different clock speeds. That only works if the library gives the bus back.

In your User_Setup.h — the file you copy over from arduino/config/User_Setup.h — this must be present and uncommented:

#define SUPPORT_TRANSACTIONS
#define TFT_MISO 12

TFT_eSPI leaves transactions off by default on ESP8266 — it force-enables them on ESP32 and nowhere else. Without it, begin_tft_write() never calls beginTransaction, so the library sets the clock and mode once at init() and never again. The first CAN read leaves the bus at 8 MHz and the display turns to confetti; or the display leaves it at 20 MHz and the CAN controller returns rubbish. Which one you see depends on the order, which is what makes it look intermittent.

The library’s own comment says it: “Transaction support is required if other SPI devices are connected.” Both lines are already set in the config file in the repo.

Bring-up order

  1. Copy arduino/config/User_Setup.h over libraries/TFT_eSPI/User_Setup.h.
  2. Flash with nothing on GPIO16 yet. You should get the splash and then the panel. If the screen is white, that is a display problem and the CAN board is not involved — use PanelDiag.
  3. Fit the 10k pull-up, then the MCP2515. Power up with the car’s ignition off. The serial line should say $CAN,ok. If it says $CAN,absent, the chip never answered: CS wiring, the pull-up, or power.
  4. Ignition on. Frames should start; $CAR,<kmh>,<rpm>,<ps>,<peak>,<volts>,<ign> arrives every five seconds. If $CAN,ok but no $CAR, you are on the wrong pair or the wrong bitrate.
  5. Drive it. Check the speed against the dash, then check torque goes negative on overrun — foot off, still in gear. That is the one on-road test that proves the 0x0A8 decode, which is the one that has never been verified.
  6. Check the battery reading against a multimeter. Within about 0.2 V.
  7. Only then fit the MPU-6050.

Where to look when something is wrong

Every file has this list at the top of it as well. One concern per file, so a symptom points at a place.

  • hud_can.h — the MCP2515 itself. Nothing about BMW. Registers, bit timing, listen-only, the drain. Nothing arrives at all → here.
  • hud_car.h — what the five E60 frames mean. One function per ID, each with its own source note and sanity limits. Compiles on a PC, so it is unit-tested. One field wrong and the rest fine → here.
  • theme_dash.h — the three-band layout, and the coordinates straight out of the HUD Bench export. Something in the wrong place, or old digits showing through → here.
  • hud_arrows.h — the manoeuvre glyphs, including the roundabout and its three geometry rules. One manoeuvre looks wrong → here.
  • hud_imu.h — the gyroscope: pins, levelling, bias. Heading drifts or the map rotates while driving straight → here.
  • NavHud.ino — wiring the above together, and deciding which of the phone or the car owns the screen.
  • test/ — make check runs fourteen groups on a PC, including an MCP2515 simulator built from the datasheet’s register map. The driver talks to a chip nobody can watch, so that is the only place its register protocol is ever checked.
